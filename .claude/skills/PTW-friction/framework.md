# Play to Win (PTW) Framework Reference

This is the complete reference for the **Play to Win** strategy framework by A.G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin (Harvard Business Review Press, 2013). All workshop participants and the facilitator use this as their shared knowledge base.

> *"Strategy is an integrated set of choices that uniquely positions the firm in its industry so as to create sustainable advantage and superior value relative to the competition."* — Lafley & Martin

Strategy is **choice**. Specifically, it is making explicit decisions about what to do — and what not to do. A claim that does not exclude something is not a strategic choice.

---

## The Strategy Choice Cascade

PTW expresses strategy as five linked choices, ordered from most abstract (top) to most concrete (bottom). Each level both **informs** the level below and **validates** the level above.

```
                  1. WINNING ASPIRATION
                  (purpose / definition of winning)
                            │
                            ▼
                  2. WHERE TO PLAY
                  (chosen playing field) 
                            │
                            ▼
                  3. HOW TO WIN
                  (recipe for winning there)
                            │
                            ▼
                  4. CAPABILITIES
                  (what we must be able to do)
                            │
                            ▼
                  5. MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS
                  (what builds & sustains the capabilities)
```

The cascade is not a one-pass exercise. Martin: *"Toggle back and forth among the five boxes."* The middle two boxes — Where to Play and How to Win — are the heart of strategy. The top box gives direction; the bottom two are the reality check that exposes whether the middle is real or wishful.

## The Five Choices

### 1. WINNING ASPIRATION
**What it is**: The purpose of the enterprise — its motivating definition of winning. A future-oriented statement focused outward, on winning with the most important customers and against the very best competitors.

**Core property: STABILITY**
- Aspirations should remain relatively stable over time so they can consistently align organizational activity.
- They are refined periodically, not rewritten each cycle.

**Quality criteria**:
- **Outward-facing**: about customers won and competitors beaten, not internal goals.
- **Specific to winning, not participating**: "to participate" is not an aspiration. *"You might be wrong"* is part of playing to win — hedged participation is the absence of strategy.
- **Inspiring but disciplined**: aspirational without crossing into fantasy that the rest of the cascade cannot support.

**What an aspiration is NOT**:
- A vision statement that any company in the industry could equally claim.
- A financial target ("doubling revenue by 2030"). That is a measure, not an aspiration.
- A mission about purpose alone with no notion of winning.

---

### 2. WHERE TO PLAY
**What it is**: The chosen playing field. The set of choices that **narrow** the competitive arena — across geographies, customer/consumer segments, channels, product categories, and vertical stages of the industry.

**Core property: DELIBERATE NARROWING**

A real Where-to-Play choice excludes more than it includes. If the answer is essentially "everywhere we can," it is not a choice. We are explicitly paranoid on this choice to be narrow, precise and exact, not ambigue. We must make hard choices. We are afraid of small market share. This does not mean we are looking to dominate the world. We simply want to precise on the "where to play". If we only serve 500 customers that's fine. But we want to define the exact narrow "where to play" that we can dominate. This is the basis of being different and unique. This is the anchor of why we can win at all.  

**Dimensions to choose across**:
- Geography (which countries / regions)
- Product category (which categories, which segments within them)
- Consumer / customer segment (which buyers, which end users)
- Channel (which distribution / go-to-market paths)
- Vertical stage (which parts of the value chain)

**Three Where-to-Play pitfalls** (Lafley/Martin):
1. **Refusing to choose** — trying to play everywhere at once.
2. **Buying out of an inherited bad choice** — using M&A or investment to mask a structurally unattractive position rather than choosing differently.
3. **Accepting the current playing field as inevitable** — treating where the company already is as something other than a choice that must be re-justified.

**Quality test**: Can you state clearly *where you are choosing not to play* — and what that costs?

---

### 3. HOW TO WIN
**What it is**: The recipe for winning **on the chosen playing field**. Not how to win generally — how to win in the specific Where-to-Play. This is the bridge between Where-to-Play and Capabilities.

**The two classic generic logics** (Porter, adopted by Lafley/Martin):
- **Cost leadership**: deliver comparable value at a structurally lower cost.
- **Differentiation**: deliver unique value that customers will pay a premium for.

In practice, How to Win is more specific than these labels — it names the actual mechanism: *"win by being the lowest-cost private-label supplier in personal care that retailers can rely on for category management,"* not just "low cost."

**Quality criteria**:
- **Tied to a specific Where-to-Play**: a How-to-Win that "works anywhere" works nowhere.
- **Customer-relevant**: the way of winning matters to the customers in the chosen segments.
- **Different from competitors**: a How-to-Win that competitors would describe identically is not a choice.

**The fit test**: *"Great strategies allow a fit between the where-to-play and how-to-win choices that makes the company stronger."* If swapping the Where-to-Play would not require rewriting the How-to-Win, the fit is too loose.

---

### 4. CAPABILITIES
**What it is**: The set and configuration of capabilities required to win in the chosen way, in the chosen field. Not a complete inventory of everything the company does — the **reinforcing system** of activities that delivers the How-to-Win.

**Core property: REINFORCING SYSTEM**
- Capabilities are chosen as a system. Each capability strengthens the others.
- The point is not to be world-class at everything — only at the few capabilities the How-to-Win requires.

**The Can't / Won't Test** (Martin): A must-have capability is one competitors either:
- *Can't* match (because they lack the underlying assets, experience, or system), or
- *Won't* match (because doing so would damage their own How-to-Win in their own Where-to-Play).

A capability that competitors *could and would* easily replicate is not a strategic capability — it is just the price of entry.

**Common failure**: listing every functional competency ("we need great marketing, great engineering, great sales") rather than the specific, configured set required by *this* How-to-Win in *this* Where-to-Play.

---

### 5. MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS
**What it is**: The structures, processes, measures, and norms that build, maintain, and refresh the Capabilities — and that hold the organization to its choices.

**Why this box matters**: *"Even if you set a winning aspiration, determine where to play and how to win, and define the capabilities required, strategy can still fail — spectacularly — if you fail to establish management systems that support those choices and capabilities."* Without systems, strategy stays a wish list.

**What this includes**:
- Organizational structure and reporting lines that match the Where-to-Play
- Performance measures and incentives that reward the How-to-Win
- Capability-building systems (hiring, training, succession, R&D)
- Decision-making routines and review cadences
- Cultural norms that make the choices "the way we do things"

**Quality test**: For each Capability, name the specific Management System that builds and sustains it. If a Capability has no Management System behind it, it is aspirational, not real.

## Relationships Between the Five Choices

```
        WINNING ASPIRATION
                │
        (sets criteria for)
                │
                ▼
        WHERE TO PLAY  ←──────┐
                │             │ (test: do these choices
        (selects field for)   │  serve the aspiration?)
                │             │
                ▼             │
        HOW TO WIN  ──────────┤
                │             │ (test: does this fit
        (defines need for)    │  this WTP — not generic?)
                │             │
                ▼             │
        CAPABILITIES  ────────┤
                │             │ (test: can't / won't?)
        (must be built by)    │
                │             │
                ▼             │
        MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS ───┘ (test: does each capability
                                have a system behind it?)
```

**Two flows** make the cascade work:

- **Downward (informs)**: each box sets the criteria for the next. Aspiration tells you *what to optimize* in Where-to-Play. Where-to-Play tells you *what game* the How-to-Win must win. How-to-Win tells you *which* Capabilities. Capabilities tell you *which* Management Systems.

- **Upward (validates)**: each box reality-checks the boxes above it. If no Management System can sustain the required Capability, the Capability isn't real — which means the How-to-Win is fantasy, which means the Where-to-Play is unsupported, which means the Aspiration is hollow.

**Coherence test**: A coherent cascade has every box answering questions raised by the box above and resolved by the box below. Martin calls a single-box "strategy" *delusional*; a three-box answer (skipping Capabilities and Management Systems) *lazy*.

**Toggle principle**: First-pass answers in any box are working hypotheses. Real strategy work happens in the iteration: tightening Where-to-Play until How-to-Win becomes specific, refining How-to-Win until Capabilities become namable, defining Capabilities until Management Systems become designable — and going back up to revise when the lower boxes cannot bear the weight.

## Reverse Engineering: "What Would Have to Be True?"

A separate but inseparable discipline. The cascade tells you **what you are choosing**. Reverse Engineering tells you **what must be true about the world for those choices to be the winning ones**.

> *"The most valuable question in strategy is not 'what is true?' but 'what would have to be true?'"* — Roger Martin

### Why this question is different from "what is true?"

- *"What is true?"* mixes **logic** and **data**. People disagree on data, get stuck on interpretation, and the conversation locks into entrenched positions.
- *"What would have to be true?"* asks only about **logic**: under what conditions would this be a good choice? Teams almost always agree on the logic before they argue about the data.

This is the move that separates strategy *advocacy* ("here is why we are right") from strategy *as testable hypothesis* ("here is what we are betting must be true").

### The standard four dimensions to reverse-engineer

For any candidate set of choices (the status quo, or a proposed alternative), ask what would have to be true about each:

1. **Industry** — Is the industry structurally attractive enough for these choices to pay off? Have segment structures or competitive intensity changed in ways that break the bet?
2. **Customers / Channels** — Do the chosen customers and channels actually behave the way the strategy assumes? Will they continue to?
3. **Relative position** — Will the chosen Capabilities and cost structures actually deliver the advantage the How-to-Win promises?
4. **Competitors** — Will competitors respond — or fail to respond — in the ways the strategy assumes? What competitive countermove would break this?

### Barriers to Choice (BTC)

Once the conditions for a possibility to be a winning choice are listed, identify which conditions feel **least likely to be true**. Those are the **Barriers to Choice** — the conditions that, if they don't hold, defeat the strategy.

The team's job is not to debate whether the strategy "is right." The team's job is to:
1. Agree on which conditions must be true.
2. Identify which of those feel least likely (the BTCs).
3. Design the cheapest, fastest test to learn whether the BTCs are actually true — *before* full commitment.

This converts strategy into a sequence of tested hypotheses rather than a single bet placed in the dark.

### Reverse-engineering the status quo

A critical move: don't only reverse-engineer alternatives. **Reverse-engineer the current strategy too.** Ask: *"What would have to be true for the current strategy to be the winning choice going forward?"*

This puts the status quo and alternatives on the same footing. Without it, the status quo wins by default — judged on what it has produced — while alternatives are judged on what they would have to produce. That is not a fair contest. The status quo must defend itself by the same logic as everything else.

### Strategy as canary in the coal mine

Once the conditions are listed, they become an ongoing monitoring system. Martin: *"Think of your WWHTBT as your canary in the coal mine."* Each condition becomes a watch-item. As long as the conditions hold, *power forward*. When one stops being true, that is the signal to revisit the cascade — not when financial results have already deteriorated.

## Common Failure Modes

### The Six Strategy Traps (Lafley & Martin)

| Trap | Description |
|---|---|
| **Do-It-All Strategy** | Pursuing every opportunity, serving every customer, playing on every field. Resources are diluted; nothing is excellent. The refusal to choose. |
| **Don Quixote Strategy** | Charging at unwinnable battles. Competing where the firm cannot realistically win — usually attacking a strong competitor head-on, on their terms. |
| **Waterloo Strategy** | Committing to a strategy without considering competitive countermoves. Treating competitors as static. The strategy works on paper, then collapses on first contact with a real competitor. |
| **Something-for-Everyone Strategy** | Trying to please all stakeholders — customers, employees, shareholders — without a consistent competitive stance. Produces a muddled value proposition that satisfies no one. |
| **Dreams-That-Never-Come-True Strategy** | Aspirations and Where-to-Play / How-to-Win choices look great, but Capabilities and Management Systems are missing. Strategy as wish list. |
| **Programme-of-the-Month Strategy** | Constantly shifting initiatives without deep commitment. Each new program kills the previous one before it could compound. Looks like activity; produces no advantage. |

### Cascade-specific failure patterns

| Failure | Description |
|---|---|
| Aspiration as financial target | "Double revenue by 2030" treated as the Aspiration. Targets measure progress; they don't define winning. |
| Where-to-Play as "everywhere" | The chosen field is so broad that no real How-to-Win can fit it. A sign that no real choice was made. |
| Generic How-to-Win | The way of winning could be lifted into any competitor's strategy without changing meaning. No actual choice. |
| Capabilities as functional list | Listing functions ("we need good marketing, good engineering") instead of the specific configured system the How-to-Win requires. |
| Capabilities that fail Can't/Won't | Capabilities that competitors could easily match. The price of entry, mistaken for the source of advantage. |
| Orphan Capabilities | Capabilities the cascade names but no Management System builds or sustains. Aspirational, not real. |
| Box-by-box thinking | Treating the five boxes as independent forms to fill in, never toggling back to test fit. The most common process failure. |
| Status quo immunity | Rigorously reverse-engineering alternatives but never reverse-engineering the current strategy. The status quo wins by default. |

## Quality Criteria for a Coherent PTW Strategy

A draft PTW strategy is **coherent** when:

1. **Every box answers a question raised by the box above** — Where-to-Play answers "where, given this Aspiration?"; How-to-Win answers "how, given this Where-to-Play?"; etc.
2. **Every box is validated by the box below** — each Capability has a Management System; the Capabilities together deliver the How-to-Win; the How-to-Win wins on the Where-to-Play; the Where-to-Play serves the Aspiration.
3. **The choices exclude something visible** — for each box, you can name what it is *not*, and what that exclusion costs.
4. **The fit between Where-to-Play and How-to-Win is tight** — swapping one would force rewriting the other.
5. **The Capabilities pass Can't/Won't** — competitors either can't or won't replicate them.
6. **Each Capability has a named Management System** — no orphan capabilities.
7. **The cascade has been reverse-engineered** — the conditions under which it wins are listed; the least-likely conditions (BTCs) are explicit; tests for the BTCs are designed before commitment.
8. **The status quo has been reverse-engineered too** — the current strategy has had to defend its own conditions, not just inherit the field by default.

A draft that satisfies all eight is rare on first pass. The workshop's job is to make the gaps explicit and put them on the table as the strategy work the organization actually has to do.
